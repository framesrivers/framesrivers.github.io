Diseñando en la frontera / Designing on the border

Most software assumes one language, one device, one kind of user.

I grew up in Roma, Texas, where a conversation starts in Spanish and finishes in English, where a phone is the only computer in the house, and where the nearest clinic or bank branch might be forty minutes away. That is the baseline I design against: low bandwidth, shared devices, code-switching, and people who will not tell you the form confused them — they will just leave. Designing for the border is not a niche. It is a stress test.

About

01 · Mobile app redesign

Noah's Ark

Redesigning a lost-pet recovery app for urgency, clarity, and emotional support.

Noah’s Ark helps pet owners take action when a pet goes missing. As reporting tools, pet profiles, community features, and donations accumulated, the most urgent flows became harder to find. I redesigned the experience around what people need in a stressful moment: reassurance, a clear next step, and less friction.

Role

Freelance Product Designer

Timeline

6 weeks

Scope

UX audit, research, interaction design, wireframes, prototype, testing

Team

Founder, developers, copy designer

Platform

Mobile app

DefineAuditResearchSolutionValidation

Define

Problem
statement

Noah’s Ark had many helpful features, but the urgent actions people needed during a missing-pet crisis were buried among donations, store content, and community features.

The product behaved like a broad platform when users needed reassurance, clarity, and one clear next step.

Possible
solutions

Based on the audit and research, I focused the redesign around a smaller set of priorities:

  • Make report and recovery actions visible immediately.
  • Shorten reporting with clear progress, review, and confirmation.
  • Keep community and monetization features available without competing with crisis flows.

Make the next step feel possible.

Outcome at a glance

A calmer, clearer path to help bring pets home.

The redesign made urgent actions easier to find, simplified reporting, and created a more supportive tone. Moderated prototype testing with five participants showed measurable gains across completion, speed, and confidence.

Task completion

4:20 → 2:30

4.1 → 6.3

The problem

When someone loses a pet, they do not need every feature the app offers. They need clarity, urgency, and a clear next step.

Reporting a lost or found pet was buried among donation buttons, store content, and community features. The product had good intentions, but its priorities did not match the user’s mindset in a crisis.

This was not just a visual problem. It was a product-priority problem.

My role

I led the UX process from audit through validation: reviewing the current experience, identifying friction in the highest-priority flows, conducting interviews with pet owners, turning research into principles, and creating sketches, wireframes, prototypes, and usability tests.

I collaborated with the copy designer, founder, and developers to refine the tone and keep the redesign grounded in both user needs and product realities.

Audit

Understanding the current experience

I started with a walkthrough of the app’s most important flows: report a missing pet, report a found pet, and create or manage a pet profile. I also looked at how social feed, donations, monetization, memorial features, and gamification shaped the reporting experience.

01

Critical actions competed with secondary features

Urgent actions competed visually and structurally with donations, store content, and community features, creating hesitation and stress.

02

The structure did not match the user’s mindset

The product behaved like a platform with many features, while users in crisis needed reassurance, clarity, and a quick path to action.

03

The tone felt too harsh in key moments

Cold language appeared where people needed calm, reassurance, and a stronger sense of support.

04

Reporting felt too burdensome

The flow felt longer, heavier, and more repetitive than it needed to be during an already stressful experience.

The audit in screens

Where urgency was getting lost.

Research

Stress changes how people move through a product

I spoke with five participants recruited from Facebook pet communities: two had lost a pet in the past, one currently had a missing pet, one had an unresolved loss, and one was a general pet owner.

I also reviewed real lost-pet posts to understand how people naturally communicate when they are trying to bring a beloved pet home.

“It feels like an interrogation.”

Participant feedback on long, repetitive forms

“Did it actually submit?”

Participant feedback on unclear confirmation

The clearest tension was between crisis language like “Lost Pet Mode” and social cues like “Followers” and “Following.” That contrast created cognitive dissonance and made the product feel less aligned with the moment.

Direction

Three principles for a more supportive product

01

Put urgent actions first

The most important tasks should be the easiest to find and complete.

02

Minimize effort during anxiety

Every extra question or unclear step feels heavier in a high-emotion moment.

03

Design with empathy

The product should not only work well. It should feel like it understands the user’s situation.

From concept to structure

Move secondary features away from the critical path.

Exploration & interaction design

From a crowded platform to a clear recovery path.

I explored sketches and concepts around critical actions, navigation, reporting flows, and guidance. Secondary features moved out of the critical path so the product could support action instead of encouraging browsing.

Guided reporting flow

Clear step indicators, plainly labeled required and optional fields, a progress bar, and encouraging microcopy make the experience feel shorter and more achievable.

Location-centered search

Scannable cards prioritize a large photo, location, last-seen time, breed, and contact snippet, creating a neighborhood bulletin board instead of a gamified feed.

One place for My Pets

A simpler dashboard manages animals, lost status, and reports without duplicate fields or confusing overlap between pet and user profiles.

High fidelity & key changes

A dramatically shorter report

The new flow starts with supportive language and reduces repetition, helping owners keep moving when they are anxious.

Review before submission

A review step, stronger confirmation states, and clearer next-step guidance reduce uncertainty about whether a report is complete.

Membership off the critical path

Wallet, store, donations, and premium features still exist, but they no longer interrupt the primary lost-and-found flows.

Humanized microcopy

Labels and confirmations now sound like support: “We’re here to help you bring them home” and “Your report is live.”

The redesigned flows

High fidelity, with empathy built into the details.

Validation

The redesigned flow felt easier to understand and more comforting.

5

Moderated participants

60% → 100%

Task completion

4.1 → 6.3

Confidence out of 7

Participants advanced through the redesigned flow with confidence, asked fewer clarification questions, and described the experience as easier to understand and more comforting overall.

What I would explore next

  • Automatically generated flyers to print
  • Location-based neighborhood notifications
  • Trust scores for community members who respond
  • Expanded usability testing with more users
  • Measurement once the product is in the wild

Reflection

The hardest part was not cleaning up the interface. It was rethinking the product around what users were actually experiencing: stress, urgency, and the need for support while trying to do the right thing quickly.

That mindset shaped every design decision and pushed me to think beyond screens and flows toward what supportive product design can really look like.

02 · Healthcare website experience

Lino's Pharmacy

Pablo: making a pharmacy assistant easier to discover and use.

Lino’s Pharmacy serves Roma and Rio Grande City, Texas. Patients needed to fill prescriptions, check medication status, and contact the pharmacy, but the website did not make those paths easy to find. I redesigned how Pablo was introduced so it felt clear, useful, familiar, and trustworthy.

Freelance Product Designer

Team

Define

Problem
statement

Pablo already existed, but the pharmacy website was not doing enough to guide patients toward it. The assistant was mostly being advertised by word of mouth.

Patients had to discover Pablo, understand what he could do, and feel confident choosing him over calling, driving through, or walking in.

Possible
solutions

The design opportunity was to make a helpful service feel familiar and easy to act on:

  • Connect Pablo to real tasks like refills and medication status.
  • Make the text and call paths visible at the moment of need.
  • Use simpler language and stronger guidance for older or less confident users.

Make the helpful thing easier to find.

Outcome at a glance

Make Pablo easier to discover, understand, and use.

The redesign connected Pablo to real pharmacy tasks instead of treating it like just another feature. In early validation, 70% of users found the instructions and successfully texted or called Pablo to complete a pharmacy-related task.

The problem

Pablo already existed, but the website was not doing enough to guide patients toward it. In fact, the only way it was being advertised was by word of mouth.

Patients first had to realize Pablo was there, understand what it could do, and feel confident choosing it over calling, driving through, or walking in.

My role

I led UX from research through early validation: reviewing the website, identifying friction, speaking with patients, and translating what I learned into a clearer introduction to Pablo.

I created sketches, wireframes, and final designs that made Pablo easier to find, easier to understand, and easier to use for familiar pharmacy tasks.

The current experience

The path existed, but it was not obvious.

The existing experience had the ingredients patients needed, but not the guidance to connect them. The redesign focused on making the next step visible and familiar.

Research

Convenience matters, but familiarity and trust matter too.

“Es mas facil ir a la farmacia.”
“It’s easier to go to the pharmacy.”
“I’m always running, I need something faster than the drive-through.”
“Ya estoy vieja para apps.”
“I’m old for apps.”

Patients wanted speed without complexity. Older participants needed clarity and reassurance, while more experienced app users expected a faster, more convenient path.

Wireframes

Make the next action feel obvious.

The opportunity was to answer three simple questions before asking patients to act: who Pablo is, what he can help with, and whether to text or call.

Final solution

A clearer invitation to get help.

Connected to real tasks

Refills, medication status, and pharmacy questions give Pablo a clear reason to exist.

Clearer entry points

Patients can see when to text and when to call without searching through the page.

Simpler language

The introduction uses plain language that reduces the work of figuring out what Pablo does.

Familiar interaction

Texting and calling feel more approachable than asking patients to learn a new tool.

Early validation

The helpful path was easier to find.

70%

Found Pablo’s instructions and completed a pharmacy-related task.

2

Core tasks tested: finding instructions and checking medication status or placing an order.

Older participants still needed stronger onboarding and clearer guidance about when to text or call. That became the next opportunity, alongside more visible cues for refills and status.

What I would explore next

  • Stronger onboarding for older and less confident users
  • Clearer guidance for when to text versus call
  • More visible cues for refills and medication status
  • Broader validation with patients across both locations
  • Post-launch measurement of task completion and confidence

Reflection

The best solution was not adding more technology. It was meeting patients where they already were: with a pharmacy they know, a task they understand, and an interaction that feels familiar.

Good digital healthcare experiences can be simple, supportive, and respectful of the confidence people bring to the screen.

03 · AI inside Salesforce workflows

Workforce CRM

AI assistant for Salesforce workflows.

An exploration into how AI could help employees locate information faster, automate repetitive tasks, and get support without leaving the workflow.

Due to NDA constraints, company details, visuals, and data in this case study have been anonymized and reconstructed.

Role

Product Designer

Timeline

3 months

Type

Exploration / Discovery

Platform

Salesforce CRM

DefineDiscoverySolutionValidationReflection

Define

Problem
statement

Salesforce supported complex internal workflows, but day-to-day requests still required context switching between records, tools, and support resources.

Employees needed a faster way to locate information and get support without leaving the workflow.

Possible
solutions

The exploration focused on making AI useful, efficient, and easy to trust:

  • Keep the assistant inside the Salesforce environment.
  • Prioritize the two highest-friction journeys instead of redesigning everything.
  • Use guided conversation and structured content to help people act with confidence.

Make the workflow feel lighter.

The problem

Salesforce supported complex internal workflows, but day-to-day requests still required context switching between records, tools, and support resources. Employees were spending time locating information instead of acting on it.

The exploration asked how AI could enhance internal Salesforce workflows while remaining useful, efficient, and easy to trust.

My role

I partnered with associates, stakeholders, and managers in discovery workshops, interviewed five users with one to ten years of experience, and identified the moments where information became difficult to find.

From there I mapped and designed two guided chatbot flows, established the conversation cadence, and collaborated with content design to make each step clear.

Discovery

People did not need another place to work. They needed help inside the place they already used.

Joint FigJam workshops with associates, stakeholders, and managers surfaced where things felt confusing, inefficient, or frustrating. Five user interviews then helped identify the moments where support mattered most.

“I get anxiety when I can’t find certain answers, and I have calls piled up already.”
“I can’t help everyone at the same time.”
“Sometimes the chat is busy, and I don’t want to bother people.”

Identifying the two priority flows

Simplify the moments that caused the most confusion.

In both priority flows, users were looking for specific information, but the journey to reach it was convoluted and required several steps. Instead of reworking the entire CRM, I focused the exploration on two high-friction paths.

Solution

Guided chatbot flows inside the existing environment.

The solution optimized two sticky flows instead of asking people to learn a completely new tool. Conversation cadence, content, and step-by-step guidance worked together to help employees locate information and take action with less friction.

Stay in context

The assistant works within the Salesforce environment employees already know.

Guide step by step

Conversation cadence turns complex requests into a clear sequence of actions.

Make support accessible

Common answers become easier to reach when the support queue is busy.

Validation

The guided flows felt clear and confident to most testers.

90%

Completion rate across the two guided chatbot flows.

5

Users interviewed during discovery.

2

Priority flows scoped and tested.

Associates, stakeholders, managers, and engineers tested the prototypes. Most navigated the experience confidently; the remaining friction became a clear direction for future discovery.

For the future

  • Continue discovering additional employee needs
  • Prioritize more return-related use cases for the assistant
  • Expand validation across roles and levels of experience
  • Measure confidence and time saved after launch

Reflection

Great UX work is not always inventing something from scratch. It can be understanding how people navigate the system they already have and making that journey better.

Research, content, and design need to work hand in hand. Clear guidance and structured conversation can make a rigid framework feel more helpful and natural.
