Diseñar en la frontera / Designing on the border

Most software assumes one language, one device, one kind of user.

I grew up in Roma, Texas, where a conversation starts in Spanish and finishes in English, where a phone is the only computer in the house, and where the nearest clinic or bank branch might be forty minutes away. That is the baseline I design against: low bandwidth, shared devices, code-switching, and people who will not tell you the form confused them — they will just leave. Designing for the border is not a niche. It is a stress test.

01 · Lost-pet recovery app

Noah's Ark

Redesigning a lost-pet recovery app for urgency, clarity, and emotional support. Noah's Ark combines reporting tools, pet profiles, and a community feed, but critical actions had become hard to find when people needed them most.

Role

Freelance Product Designer

Timeline

6 weeks

Team

Founder, developers, copy designer

Platform

Mobile app

The problem

Noah's Ark had grown into a busy product with reporting, pet profiles, a community feed, donations, and a store. The problem was not a lack of features. It was that urgent actions were competing with everything else.

For someone looking for a missing pet, a social feed and a crisis flow created cognitive dissonance. Reporting was also longer than it needed to be, and the tone felt harsh at the exact moment users needed reassurance.

My role

I audited the existing product, interviewed pet owners, mapped the core flows, created design principles, built sketches, wireframes, and prototypes, and tested the redesign with users.

The work focused on three core flows: report a missing pet, report a found pet, and create or manage a pet profile. I collaborated with the founder, developers, and copy designer throughout.

The current experience

The product asked people to think like social users during a crisis.

The audit showed the conflict clearly: a social feed, profile management, memorial features, and monetization were all sharing the same space as the actions people needed most. These screens are the evidence behind the product-priority problem.

Research

I interviewed five participants recruited from Facebook pet communities: two had previously lost a pet, one currently had a missing pet, one had experienced an unresolved loss, and one was a general pet owner. I also reviewed real lost-pet posts.

The clearest pattern was simple: stress amplifies every interaction. People need reassurance, and real recovery behavior is speedy, functional, and community-first.

Design principles

Put urgent actions first. Make the next right action obvious.

Minimize effort during anxiety. Remove unnecessary decisions and repetition.

Design with empathy, not just efficiency. The interface should help people feel supported.

Exploration and interaction design

Step 01

Sketches

Explored a home experience organized around critical actions, with secondary features moved out of the critical path.

Step 02

Wireframes

Added a step indicator, separated required and optional fields, gave location more importance, and introduced a review step before submission.

Step 03

Prototype testing

Tested missing-pet and found-pet reporting with five participants, then refined the flow, language, and confirmations around what people actually needed.

The redesign

The final direction used a shorter guided reporting flow, clearer progress and required/optional labels, a map-centered lost-and-found search, scannable pet cards, and a simpler My Pets dashboard. Monetization and membership moved away from the critical path. The tone became supportive instead of clinical or alarming.

Outcome

60% → 100%

Task completion for the tested reporting flows.

4:20 → 2:30

Average task completion time.

4.1 → 6.3/7

Confidence rating after completing the tasks.

Validation

The redesign made urgent work easier to start and finish.

I ran moderated usability sessions comparing the original experience with the redesign. Participants completed missing-pet and found-pet reporting tasks, asked fewer clarification questions, and described the new flow as easier and more comforting.

What changed

Before: urgent actions competed with donation, store, community, and membership features.

After: reporting became shorter, more guided, and more supportive, with search and pet management organized around recovery.

What I learned

The hardest part was not cleaning up the interface. It was rethinking the product around stress, urgency, and support. When the user is worried, every extra question feels bigger.

Next opportunities

Future work could explore auto-generated flyers, neighborhood notifications, trust signals, expanded testing, and real-world recovery outcomes while considering scams, shelter partnerships, monetization, and crisis ethics.

02 · Bilingual healthcare AI

Lino's Pharmacy Chatbot

An independent pharmacy in the Valley wanted an AI assistant for refills and questions. The design work was mostly about restraint: what the bot is allowed to answer, and how visibly it hands you to a person.

Role

UX & conversation design

Timeline

14 weeks

Team

Pharmacist, 2 devs, me

Tools

Figma, Dovetail

Usuario

¿Ya está lista la medicina de mi mamá?

Asistente

Sí — lista desde las 9:40 AM. Fuente: registro de resurtido

User

Can she take this with her blood pressure pill?

Handoff

That is a question for the pharmacist. I am sending this chat to Lino now — he answers in about 10 minutes.

The problem

The phone rang all day with three questions: is my refill ready, how much will it cost, and can I take this with that. Most callers preferred Spanish; the staff answering were two people also filling prescriptions.

Twelve interviews told me the fear was specific — not “I don't trust AI,” but “if it is wrong about my mother's medication, who do I call.”

What I did

Wrote the scope as a visible contract: the bot handles status, hours, cost, and location. Anything clinical routes to the pharmacist with the conversation attached, and says so before you finish typing.

Language follows the user mid-sentence instead of a toggle they have to find, and every answer shows its source: “from your refill record, updated 9:40 AM.”

Outcome

−46%

Inbound calls about refill status in the first month.

68%

Of chats started in Spanish and never needed a toggle.

100%

Of clinical questions routed to the pharmacist, by design.

03 · AI inside a sales CRM

CRM AI

Reps were being handed AI summaries and next-step suggestions they had no way to check. Adoption was low and the complaint was always the same: where did this come from?

Role

Product designer

Timeline

12 weeks

Team

PM, 4 engineers, me

Tools

Figma, Amplitude

Suggested next step AI draft

Send pricing for the 40-seat tier before Friday — procurement asked twice on the last call.

Because of

Call transcript, Sep 12 (14:06) · Email thread “Seat count” · Stage: Evaluation

Accept Edit Dismiss

Key screen: every suggestion carries its sources and stays editable

The problem

The model was good. The presentation was not. Suggestions arrived as finished sentences with no provenance, no way to correct them, and no memory of being dismissed — so reps stopped looking.

Nine ride-alongs showed the real workflow: reps trust their own notes first, and anything they cannot trace they ignore.

What I did

Made every suggestion cite the calls, emails, and fields it came from, and made editing the default action rather than a hidden one. Dismissals now teach the system instead of disappearing.

Labeled AI output as a draft everywhere it appears, so nothing reaches a customer without a person having read it.

Outcome

3.1×

Weekly use of AI suggestions after the provenance redesign.

72%

Of accepted drafts were edited first — exactly the intent.

9

Ride-alongs that shaped the final interaction model.
